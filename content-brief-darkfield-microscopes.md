### H1: Darkfield Microscopes

**Intro paragraph (≈60–80 words)**
Darkfield microscopy makes the invisible visible. By directing light obliquely at the specimen — so that only scattered and diffracted light enters the objective — the technique renders transparent, unstained specimens as brilliantly lit objects against a black background. The result is striking contrast without staining or fixation, preserving specimen integrity and enabling live-cell observation. Evident Scientific's research and clinical microscope platforms support darkfield illumination across a range of configurations.

---

### H2: How Darkfield Illumination Works

**Copy guidance (≈150 words)**
Cover the optical principle clearly:
- Standard brightfield directs light straight through the specimen and into the objective; structures are visible only if they absorb or refract light.
- Darkfield inserts a patch stop (opaque disk) in the condenser, blocking the central cone of direct illumination. The condenser instead delivers a hollow cone of oblique light that strikes the specimen from the sides.
- Light that passes through without interacting with the sample misses the objective's numerical aperture entirely — the background stays dark.
- When light hits a structure — a cell membrane, bacterium, particle, or surface feature — it scatters into the objective. Those structures glow brightly against the dark field.
- The human eye can detect individual nanoparticles ≥30 nm because the light they scatter is far brighter than the dark background.

**Key technical note to include:**
Objective NA must be lower than the inner NA of the darkfield condenser. Dry darkfield condensers typically require objectives with NA ≤ 0.8; oil-immersion darkfield condensers can work with higher-NA objectives up to ~1.2. Mention that closing the objective's iris diaphragm (on iris-equipped objectives) also enables darkfield with standard condensers.

**Diagram / illustration suggestion:** Side-by-side ray diagrams showing brightfield vs. darkfield light paths through condenser and objective.

### H2: What Can You See with Darkfield Microscopy?

**Copy guidance (≈150–200 words)**
Use this section to convey the breadth of applications. Organize as short descriptive paragraphs or a visual card layout. Key content to include:

**Biological specimens**
Living bacteria, spirochetes, protists, algae, and blood cells are nearly transparent in brightfield but appear crisp and bright in darkfield. No staining required — ideal for motility studies and live-organism observation.

**Blood and clinical samples**
Darkfield is used clinically to observe Treponema pallidum (the causative agent of syphilis) and to detect abnormal red blood cell morphology without disrupting sample integrity.

**Nanoparticles and colloidal suspensions**
Individual gold, silver, and silica nanoparticles (10–100 nm) produce characteristic scattering spectra under darkfield illumination. Used in single-particle tracking and localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) studies.

**Surface quality and industrial inspection**
Scratches, polishing marks, corrosion pits, inclusions, and surface contamination on metals, glass, and semiconductors scatter light strongly in darkfield, making defect detection faster and more reliable.

**Unstained tissue sections**
Fibrous structures — collagen, elastin, and other connective tissue components — are inherently birefringent or reflective and appear bright in darkfield even without staining.

### H2: Choosing the Right Darkfield Configuration

**Copy guidance (≈150 words)**
This is where you help buyers match the technique to the right platform — and start channeling toward Evident products.

**Dry darkfield condenser (NA 0.8–0.95)**
Best for lower-magnification work (2×–40×) with standard glass slides and dry objectives. Works with most compound upright microscopes without special sample preparation. No immersion oil needed.

**Oil-immersion darkfield condenser (NA 1.2–1.4)**
Required for high-magnification darkfield at 40×–100× with high-NA oil objectives. Delivers brighter, higher-resolution darkfield images. Requires immersion oil between the condenser top lens and the slide underside.

**Inverted darkfield**
Available for some inverted platforms to enable darkfield observation of cells growing in culture dishes or multi-well plates. Useful for live-cell experiments where a standard condenser cannot be used.

**Stereo darkfield**
Fiber-optic ring lights or coaxial darkfield illuminators enable darkfield-like surface illumination on stereo microscopes for industrial and forensic inspection.

**NA compatibility rule:** The outer NA of the condenser's hollow cone must exceed the NA of the objective being used. If an objective has a built-in iris diaphragm, closing it reduces its effective NA and enables darkfield with a standard condenser.

### H2: Frequently Asked Questions

Include the following Q&As (≈40–60 words each answer):

**Q1: What is darkfield microscopy used for?**
Darkfield microscopy is used to observe transparent, unstained, or low-contrast specimens — including live bacteria, blood cells, nanoparticles, and surface defects on materials — by illuminating the sample obliquely so only scattered light enters the objective. The result is a bright specimen against a black background, maximizing contrast without staining.

**Q2: What is the difference between darkfield and brightfield microscopy?**
In brightfield microscopy, direct light passes through the specimen and into the objective; the background is bright and the specimen appears dark if it absorbs light. In darkfield, a condenser patch stop blocks direct light so only obliquely scattered light enters the objective. The background is dark and even very thin, transparent specimens glow brightly.

**Q3: Do I need a special microscope for darkfield?**
You do not need a dedicated darkfield-only microscope. Most research-grade compound upright and inverted microscopes — including Evident Scientific's BX and IX platforms — accept darkfield condensers as accessories. The primary requirement is objective NA compatibility: the objective's NA must be lower than the condenser's inner NA to prevent direct light from entering the objective.

**Q4: Can I use darkfield with oil-immersion objectives?**
Yes, but it requires an oil-immersion darkfield condenser (NA 1.2–1.4). You apply immersion oil between the condenser's top lens and the underside of the slide. High-NA oil darkfield condensers enable sharper, brighter darkfield images at 40×–100× and are essential for viewing very small structures like bacteria and nanoparticles at high resolution.

**Q5: What is the smallest thing visible with darkfield microscopy?**
Because darkfield detects scattered light rather than absorption, it is sensitive to objects far below the Abbe diffraction limit in terms of detection (though not resolution). Individual nanoparticles as small as ~30 nm can be detected due to the brightness of their scattering against the dark background. Resolution remains diffraction-limited, typically ~200 nm with visible light.

**Q6: Is darkfield microscopy used clinically?**
Yes. Darkfield microscopy has historically been used in clinical microbiology to detect Treponema pallidum in syphilis chancre samples, where the organism's characteristic corkscrew motility is visible without staining. Clinical labs also use darkfield to observe parasites and examine live blood morphology.
